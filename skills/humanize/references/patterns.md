# Pattern library

These are evidence, not a find-and-replace list. Any phrase here can be fine when it carries precise meaning or is part of how the writer really talks. Remove it when it stalls, inflates, generalizes, or performs insight instead of delivering it.

Each entry gives the move, examples, and the fix. Patterns are grouped by where they show up.

---

## A. Framing: how a point gets introduced

### A1. Secret-insight framing

Dressing an ordinary point up as hidden knowledge, with the writer as the lone person who sees it.

- "What nobody tells you about pricing..."
- "This is the part most teams skip."
- "Here's what everyone gets wrong."
- "The thing no one is talking about."

**Fix:** state the claim and back it up. "Most teams skip X" is fine only if the writer can show that they do.

### A2. Tee-ups and announcements

A sentence whose only job is to advertise the next sentence.

- "Here's the kicker."
- "Here's where it gets interesting."
- "Let me explain."
- "Which brings me to the real point."
- "I want to be precise here, because this is where people trip up."

**Fix:** delete it and say the thing. Announcing that you are about to be specific adds nothing. Being specific does.

### A3. Throat-clearing and staged candor

Openers that delay the subject or manufacture intimacy before a routine claim.

- "Here's the thing."
- "Let me be clear."
- "I'll be honest with you."
- "Honestly?" / "Let's be real."
- "The uncomfortable truth is..."
- "Imagine a world where..." / "Picture this:"

**Fix:** start with the subject. Keep a real admission ("I got this wrong for two years") because it carries information.

### A4. Generic scene-setting

Openings that could start any article on any topic.

- "In today's fast-paced digital world..."
- "As AI continues to reshape the landscape..."
- "In an era of rapid change..."
- "Whether you're a startup founder or a Fortune 500 executive..."

**Fix:** start with the subject or the reader's actual situation.

### A5. Assistant residue

Chat-assistant habits that leak into finished writing.

- "Great question!" / "Certainly!" / "Absolutely!"
- "I hope this helps." / "Let me know if you'd like me to expand on any of these."
- "Here's a comprehensive overview of..."
- "As an AI language model..."

**Fix:** delete. Finished writing does not talk about itself being written.

### A6. Definition openers

Starting with a dictionary-style description of the thing instead of why anyone should care.

- "I built Acme, an open-source platform that enables teams to..."
- "Project X is a comprehensive solution designed to..."
- "This post covers..."

**Fix:** open with the problem, the reason, or the moment that started it, when the source gives you one. For example: "I got tired of rebuilding the same report every Monday, so I built Acme." Put the definition in a short second sentence. In reference docs and READMEs a definition opener is fine (see contexts.md).

## B. Structure: sentence and paragraph shapes

These survive word swaps. Check the shape, not just the vocabulary.

### B1. Binary reframes

Inventing a weaker claim just to knock it down.

- "It's not a tool. It's a teammate."
- "The question isn't speed, it's trust."
- "You don't have a data problem. You have a decision problem."
- "It's not just X, it's Y."

**Fix:** state the stronger claim directly: "Trust matters more than speed here." Keep a contrast only when it corrects a belief the reader actually holds.

### B2. Negative stacking

Lists of what something is not, landing on what it is.

- "Not a dashboard. Not a report. A system."
- "No setup. No cost. Just results."
- "No key, no login."

**Fix:** write one complete sentence: "It needs no key or login." This applies even when each item is concrete and true.

### B3. Fake question-and-answer

A declarative sentence chopped in two for false momentum.

- "The biggest risk? Adoption."
- "The result? Pipeline doubled."
- "What if I told you..." / "Think about it:" / "Plot twist:"

**Fix:** join it: "Adoption is the biggest risk." Keep real questions the reader should actually consider.

### B4. Colon reveals

A noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase "reveal".

- "The best part: it learns."
- "The secret ingredient: consistency."

**Fix:** write a normal sentence. Colons are for lists, labels, definitions, and quotations.

### B5. Dramatic fragments and mechanical stacks

Short sentences in identical molds, stacked for drama.

- "You plan. You build. You ship."
- "That's it. That's the whole thing."
- "Faster. Cheaper. Better."
- "And it worked. And it scaled. And it stuck."

**Fix:** combine into sentences that say how the parts relate. Keep a single fragment that clearly belongs to the writer.

### B6. Forced triads

Grouping in threes by reflex, often with the third item repeating the first two.

- "clear, concise, and compelling"
- "people, process, and technology" (when only one is discussed)

**Fix:** keep the items that are real and distinct. Two is a fine number. So is four.

### B7. Template paragraphs

Every paragraph built the same way: claim, explanation, one-line takeaway. Every paragraph the same length. Every section opening with a rhetorical question.

**Fix:** let content set the shape. Vary length and entry point only where it helps.

### B8. False balance

Reflexive both-sides hedging that commits to nothing.

- "While AI offers many benefits, it also presents challenges."
- "There are pros and cons to every approach."

**Fix:** say which benefits, which challenges, and what the writer thinks. If there is no position, cut the sentence.

### B9. Range constructions

"From X to Y" spans that sound comprehensive without saying anything.

- "from startups to enterprises"
- "from strategy to execution"

**Fix:** name who or what is actually covered, or drop the range.

### B10. Stuffed sentences

One sentence carrying an appositive, a colon list, and a trailing "and" clause. The reader has to hold three ideas at once.

- "I built X, an open-source set of tools for Y that removes Z: A, B, and C, and it also does D."

**Fix:** one idea per sentence. Split at the appositive and at the "and". Keep the colon list only if it is the point of the sentence.

## C. Claims: how importance and evidence are handled

### C1. Importance labels

Telling the reader something matters instead of showing why.

- "This is a pivotal moment."
- "This stands as a testament to..."
- "plays a crucial role in"
- "This distinction matters."
- "That last part is bigger than it sounds."
- "The key takeaway here is..."

**Fix:** state the fact, mechanism, or consequence and let the reader weigh it.

### C2. Hollow analysis clauses

Trailing "-ing" clauses that pose as interpretation.

- "...highlighting the team's commitment to quality."
- "...underscoring the importance of data."
- "...reflecting a broader shift in the industry."
- "...showcasing its versatility."

**Fix:** replace with a real consequence ("...so analysts no longer rebuild the report by hand") or cut it.

### C3. Reaction narration

Telling the reader how to feel about a fact.

- "Let that sink in."
- "Read that again."
- "That's unheard of."
- "That's the whole game."
- "And that's when it clicked."

**Fix:** let the fact land. Add a consequence only if it is new information.

### C4. Vague authority

Borrowed credibility with no source.

- "Experts agree..."
- "Studies show..."
- "Industry reports suggest..."
- "It's widely recognized that..."

**Fix:** name the source and what it found. If the writer has none, cut or soften the claim, and flag it for the user. Never invent a source.

### C5. Hedge stacking

Piling qualifiers until the claim disappears.

- "This may potentially help to somewhat improve..."
- "It could arguably be seen as..."

**Fix:** keep the one hedge that reflects real uncertainty. Drop the rest.

### C6. Sales and transformation language

Promises with no mechanism.

- "Unlock your full potential."
- "Supercharge your workflow."
- "Take your business to the next level."
- "Seamless, end-to-end, holistic solution."

**Fix:** name the action and the supported outcome: "Cuts month-end close from nine days to four."

### C7. Straw competitors

Making your thing look good by describing everyone else's as bad, with nothing to back it up.

- "Most tools in this space are clunky and slow."
- "Unlike other solutions, ours actually works."
- "Most tools that do this hand back text that sounds like nobody."

**Fix:** turn it into the writer's own motive when that is implied ("I got tired of tools that..."). Or name the specific alternative and the specific difference. Or cut it. A first-person reason needs no evidence. A claim about a whole market does.

## D. Vocabulary

### D1. Stock AI vocabulary

Avoid by default. Keep a word when it names something precise. A "robust" estimator in statistics is fine, and so is a "framework" that is literally software.

delve, tapestry, realm, beacon, landscape (figurative), ecosystem (figurative), journey (figurative), navigate (figurative), testament, pivotal, paramount, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, nuanced (as filler), transformative, revolutionary, game-changer, paradigm shift, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, ever-evolving, seamless, holistic, synergy, robust, leverage, utilize, facilitate, foster, empower, streamline, elevate, embark, harness, supercharge, unlock, unleash, spearhead, bolster, underscore, showcase, "move the needle", "dive deep", "deep dive", "this changes everything", "this is huge"

### D2. Filler phrases

Cut when they delay the point.

it's worth noting, it's important to note, needless to say, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, in the world of, the reality is, the truth is, the fact of the matter is, in terms of, with regard to, in order to, due to the fact that, going forward, moving forward, in this article, in this section we will, let's dive in, let's explore, without further ado

### D3. Often-empty adverbs

Cut when they add nothing. Keep when they carry emphasis, contrast, uncertainty, or the writer's spoken rhythm.

just, really, very, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, basically, fundamentally, essentially, importantly, crucially, significantly, inherently, inevitably, incredibly, quietly, seamlessly

### D4. Inflated verbs and abstract nouns

- "serves as" / "acts as" / "functions as" when "is" works
- "has the ability to" for "can"
- "made a decision" for "decided"
- "conducted an analysis" for "analyzed"
- "drove improvements in efficiency" with no named process or number

**Fix:** plain verb, human subject, concrete object.

### D5. Synonym cycling

Renaming one thing for variety: "the platform... the solution... the tool... the system". Readers wonder whether these are four things.

**Fix:** pick the right word and repeat it.

## E. Endings

### E1. Fake-profound kickers

A final line that turns the point into an aphorism or mic drop.

- "The future isn't coming. It's already here."
- "Data is the new oil."
- "In the end, it's all about people."

**Fix:** delete it. Do not write a better metaphor. End on the clearest concrete sentence already there.

### E2. Recap endings

"In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", "To sum up", or a last paragraph that restates the piece.

**Fix:** end on the last concrete point, decision, or next action. Long reports with a formal summary section are the exception (see contexts.md).

### E3. Engagement bait

- "What do you think? Drop a comment below."
- "Agree or disagree?"
- "Follow for more."

**Fix:** cut unless the user asked for a call to action. If they did, make it a specific question tied to the post.

## F. Formatting and punctuation

- **Em dashes and en dashes.** None. Replace with a period, comma, colon, parentheses, or "and"/"but". Write number ranges as "2019 to 2021".
- **Semicolons.** None. Split into two sentences, or join with "and" or "but".
- **Long sentences.** Keep each sentence to 20 words or fewer. Split at the "and", the "which", or the appositive.
- **Emojis.** None unless requested or native to the writer's voice. Never as bullet markers or heading decorations by default.
- **Decorative bold.** Do not bold random phrases mid-sentence. Bold is for labels and scannable key terms.
- **Bullet creep.** Two or three related sentences read better as prose than as bullets.
- **Heading creep.** No headings over sections that are one or two sentences long.
- **Title Case Everywhere.** Use sentence case for headings unless the house style says otherwise.
- **Colons.** Sentence case after a colon unless grammar, a proper noun, a title, or code requires a capital.
- **Curly vs straight quotes.** Match what the rest of the document uses. Do not mix.

---

## The core test

If a sentence exists to build anticipation for the next sentence, cut it and say the next thing. Do not narrate the reader's reaction. Do not promise that something is important. Put the evidence down and let it work.
